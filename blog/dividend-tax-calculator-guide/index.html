Dividend Tax Calculator: What Will You Actually Keep?

Your ETF deposited C$300. Your brokerage app says “income.” But what portion is taxable, and what does that mean for the money you can actually spend?

TotalYields editorial guide · Updated November 13, 2026 · Examples are hypothetical

Alex had a simple plan. His investments would send him a little cash every month, and he would use that money to help with bills. Then tax season arrived. The number in his brokerage app did not match the categories on his tax slip.

Alex: “My ETF paid C$300. Do I just put C$300 into a dividend tax calculator?”

Me: “Not so fast. First we need to know what the C$300 was. An ETF distribution can contain several kinds of income, and they may not be taxed the same way.”

That distinction is the whole reason for this guide. Our existing Canadian after-tax dividend estimator helps you explore an estimated result. This article does something different: it shows you which numbers belong in a calculator, what your tax slip is telling you, and why a distribution is not automatically a dividend.

First: a distribution is a payment, not a tax category

When a fund pays you, the cash might include eligible Canadian dividends, other-than-eligible dividends, foreign income, interest, realized capital gains, or return of capital (ROC). Some ETFs combine multiple components in one distribution. Your brokerage deposit alone does not tell you the final tax character. Canadian fund trusts commonly report allocated amounts on a T3 slip; certain corporate fund distributions may be reported on a T5. See the CRA source below.

Keep two separate questions in mind.

Cash flow: How much was deposited into my account?
Tax character: What does my annual tax reporting say the distribution contained?

Why the Canadian dividend tax credit changes the math

For eligible dividends from taxable Canadian corporations, Canadian tax rules use a gross-up and a dividend tax credit. That means your taxable dividend amount is not necessarily the same as the cash received. The credit can offset some tax. Other-than-eligible dividends have different calculations, and foreign dividends generally do not qualify for this Canadian credit. Provincial or territorial rules and your other income also affect the final result. The CRA explains the applicable rules and tax-slip boxes in its official guidance.

For example, the CRA's T5 guide describes a 38% gross-up for eligible dividends under the rules covered there. If the eligible-dividend portion of Alex's hypothetical payment is C$120, the corresponding grossed-up amount is C$165.60. That is not a tax bill. It is an input into the income-tax calculation, alongside credits and the rest of his return. We are not applying an invented flat “Canadian dividend tax rate” to the full C$300.

Important: Tax rules can change by year, province and circumstances. A simplified article calculator cannot accurately reproduce a complete Canadian tax return, dividend tax credit calculation, foreign tax credits, withholding or capital gains reporting. For a filing-quality estimate, use current CRA guidance or tax software and your actual slips.

The simplified calculation is (cash − assumed ROC) × assumed effective rate. It excludes tax credits, gains inclusion rules, withholding, cost-base effects and tax on any separate sales. ROC may have future tax consequences.

Notice what happens if you change the assumed ROC portion. The model reserves less immediate cash for taxes, but that does not mean ROC is free money. In Canada, ROC generally reduces adjusted cost base, which can increase a future capital gain. Special rules apply if adjusted cost base reaches zero. In the US, nondividend distributions generally reduce basis, with gains possible after basis reaches zero.

And what if Alex lives in the United States?

The same cash versus tax character principle applies, but the categories are different. A US taxpayer may see ordinary dividends in Form 1099-DIV box 1a, qualified dividends in box 1b (a subset of ordinary dividends), capital gain distributions in box 2a, and nondividend distributions in box 3. Qualified dividends can be subject to preferential rates if requirements are met, including applicable holding-period rules. You cannot assume every ETF payout is a qualified dividend. The IRS instructions explain the reporting categories.

For a US investor, the relevant federal and state tax result can depend on filing status, total taxable income, holding period, and the type of distribution. For a Canadian investor holding US investments, cross-border withholding and account rules add another layer. A single “North American dividend tax rate” would be convenient, but fictional.

What about TFSA, RRSP, Roth IRA and traditional IRA accounts?

Before estimating tax, identify where the ETF is held. Canadian TFSA and RRSP accounts and US Roth and traditional IRAs have different tax rules from ordinary taxable accounts. Tax treatment may depend on contributions, withdrawals, foreign withholding, residency and account-specific restrictions. Do not copy the non-registered example above into a registered account and assume the result is the same. For cross-border holdings, check the current guidance that applies to your residency and account.

One more trap: cash income is not total return

Alex: “At least the C$300 means my investment made C$300, right?”

Me: “It means C$300 was distributed. Whether you actually made money depends on what happened to the value of your investment too.”

Suppose an ETF starts at C$10,000, distributes C$1,200 over a year and ends worth C$9,100, with no contributions or sales. Ignoring tax and fees, its simple total return for this example is (C$9,100 − C$10,000 + C$1,200) ÷ C$10,000 = 3%. Cash distributed is C$1,200, but the investment did not earn a 12% total return. If the ending value were C$8,500 instead, the same C$1,200 cash distribution would accompany a −3% total return.

Those are hypothetical figures, not a real ETF's performance. Distributions can be cut; fund prices can decline; some strategies have higher fees, options exposure, leverage or other risks. Taxes can reduce your personal after-tax result further. For the deeper explanation, read Why an ETF can pay monthly and still lose value and Can you live off high-yield ETFs?.

Before you enter a number, check these five things

  1. Country and tax residency: Canada and the US do not use interchangeable dividend rules.
  2. Account type: taxable brokerage versus registered or retirement account.
  3. Distribution character: your T3/T5 or 1099-DIV categories, rather than just the broker's cash total.
  4. Other income and jurisdiction: federal and provincial/state rules affect the outcome.
  5. Investment performance: compare cash payouts with price changes, fees and taxes.

Frequently asked questions

Are ETF distributions taxed like ordinary dividends?

Not necessarily. ETF distributions can contain different tax categories, including dividends, foreign income, capital gains and return of capital. Use the actual tax reporting for your fund and jurisdiction.

Is return of capital tax-free?

It is generally not immediately taxed while it reduces adjusted cost base or basis under applicable rules, but it can affect later capital gains. Special treatment applies once basis reaches zero. “Not taxed now” does not mean “free profit.”

Can I calculate dividend tax using just the yield?

No. Yield helps describe distributions relative to price. Tax depends on distribution character, tax residency, account type and your wider tax situation.

Do Canadian eligible dividends and US qualified dividends mean the same thing?

No. They are terms from different tax systems with different eligibility and calculation rules. Do not transfer a rate or credit from one country to the other.

Why does my broker's dividend total differ from my tax slip?

A brokerage cash ledger shows deposits, while tax slips allocate taxable categories and can include adjustments such as reinvested or noncash amounts. Reconcile the full annual records before filing.

The takeaway: Start with the cash payment, but do not stop there. Identify its tax character, account and jurisdiction, then use the right calculation. Your after-tax cash flow and your total investment return are two different numbers worth tracking.
