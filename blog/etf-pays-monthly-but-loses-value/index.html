Your ETF pays C$200 a month. Are you actually making money?

The payment feels good. The falling balance feels confusing. Let’s put the two together.

By TotalYields Editorial · Published October 28, 2026 · 6-minute read

You open your brokerage app. Another C$200 payment has arrived. Nice—that’s groceries covered, or a little extra breathing room this month.

Then you look at the investment balance. You put in C$10,000. Now your ETF units are worth C$8,000.

YOU MIGHT BE THINKING“Hold on. I’m getting paid every month. How can I still be losing money?”

Because the payment and the value of your investment answer different questions. The payment tells you how much cash arrived. Your overall return tells you whether your money grew after accounting for what remains invested.

Let’s walk through it together.

“So what did I actually earn?”

Suppose you held the ETF for one year, made no extra contributions, sold no units and kept every distribution as cash. Here’s our hypothetical example:

You started withC$10,000
You collected C$200 × 12C$2,400
Your units are now worthC$8,000
Units plus cash collectedC$10,400
Your simple holding-period gain: C$400, or 4%.
Before personal taxes and trading costs. Cash is assumed to earn nothing. This is an illustration, not a forecast or an actual fund’s performance.

That C$2,400 was real cash. But C$2,000 of the benefit was offset by the decline in your units’ value. You received cash equal to 24% of your starting investment; you didn’t earn a 24% overall return.

FAIR QUESTION“And if the units fell to C$7,000 instead?”

Then C$7,000 plus C$2,400 would leave you with C$9,400 in total: a C$600 loss, or 6%, despite receiving all twelve payments.

One useful habit: compare the cash received with the change in investment value over the same period. If you use a fund’s published total-return figure, check its methodology. Those figures generally already include reinvested distributions—adding the yield again would double-count them.[1]

“But the yield went up. Isn’t that good?”

Maybe. First, check why it went up.

Imagine an ETF paying C$0.20 a unit each month. At a C$10 unit price, annualizing that payment gives 24%. At C$8, the same payment gives 30%.

You haven’t received a raise. The denominator got smaller.

Issuers can calculate yield differently. Global X, for example, annualizes its latest regular distribution and divides it by NAV. That describes the latest payment relative to fund value; it doesn’t guarantee the next twelve payments or your future return.[1]

“Where does all that cash come from?”

For a covered-call ETF, part of the answer is option premiums. The fund sells call options against its holdings and collects payments from buyers.

There’s a tradeoff. Depending on the options written, the strategy can give up some upside when holdings rise. The premiums can cushion losses, but the underlying investments can still fall substantially.[2]

THE NEXT QUESTION“Someone told me return of capital means they’re just giving my money back. Should I worry?”

It deserves a closer look, but the tax label alone doesn’t settle it.

Hamilton ETFs explains that losses can offset option gains, so a payment may be classified as return of capital for tax purposes even when the strategy generated option cash flow. That’s why it helps to distinguish where cash came from from how it is reported for tax.[3]

Look at total return, the distribution history and the strategy together. A large payment by itself can’t tell you whether the fund is preserving your wealth.

“Does that matter in my Canadian account?”

In a non-registered account, it can. Return of capital generally reduces your adjusted cost base—the amount used to calculate your gain or loss when you sell. That can increase a later capital gain or reduce a capital loss. If the adjustment would take the cost base below zero, the excess generally becomes a capital gain that year.[4]

Those cost-base rules shouldn’t be applied to registered accounts in the same way. The CRA’s guidance specifically separates TFSA and tax-deferred plan holdings from this discussion. Your account type matters.[4]

How do you calculate a monthly dividend payout?

Here's the basic monthly dividend income calculation: multiply the number of ETF units you own by the distribution paid per unit. If you own 100 units and the fund distributes C$0.20 per unit each month, that's C$20 a month, before taxes and fees. If the payment is quarterly, divide that quarter's cash by three to get a monthly average, not a promise of monthly payments.

For a yield-based estimate, multiply the invested amount by the assumed annual distribution yield, then divide by 12. For example, C$10,000 × 6% ÷ 12 = C$50 per month. This is only an estimate: fund prices and distributions can change.

Try the monthly dividend income calculator, or compare the monthly dividend yield calculator. Both calculations should be checked against the fund's current distribution details.

“I still want monthly income. What should I check?”

Start with the amount you could manage if the payment shrank.

A hypothetical 25% cut turns C$200 a month into C$150. That is C$600 less over a year. Would you trim spending, use savings or need to sell units?

Then compare total returns over matching periods and in the same currency. Read the fund’s expenses and strategy. If it uses leverage, understand that borrowing can magnify losses as well as gains. And remember that distributions can fluctuate.[1]

Try this with your own numbers: write down your monthly distribution, reduce it by 25%, then multiply the difference by twelve. A dividend-cut impact calculator is a useful next step for the CountYield toolkit; use the arithmetic here if the tool isn’t available.

“So is a high-income ETF a bad idea?”

It can serve a purpose when regular cash fits your needs. Just judge it on the whole result: the payments, the value left invested and the risks you’re taking to receive that income.

Next time a payment lands, enjoy the breathing room. Then give the rest of the investment a look too.
