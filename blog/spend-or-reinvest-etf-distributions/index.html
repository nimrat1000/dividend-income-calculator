Spend It or Reinvest It?
The ETF Income Decision Nobody Can Make From a Yield Chart

Your ETF pays you every month. Should that money help with today's bills, or go straight back into investments? Follow one investor's $200-a-month experiment and test your own numbers.

◷  9-minute read▤  October 26, 2026◇  Investing education

Alex gets $200. Now what?

Tuesday morning. Alex's phone buzzes while the coffee is still brewing. His investment account has received another ETF distribution: $200.

He could use $120 toward groceries and put the remaining $80 back into the ETF. Or reinvest the whole payment. Or spend every dollar, which sounds especially attractive after seeing the latest electricity bill.

None of those choices is automatically foolish. The problem starts when Alex assumes the biggest payment must be the best investment strategy.

Instead, he makes a plan with two separate jobs: cash for life now and capital for life later.

Our starting example: Alex owns $30,000 of a hypothetical ETF portfolio distributing at an annualized rate of 8%. That's $2,400 a year, or roughly $200 per month if the distribution level stays unchanged. A fund's distributions can rise, fall, or stop, and this calculation does not estimate total return.

One payment. Two jobs.

The simplest way to make the decision visible is to split the $200 rather than treating it like free money.

Spending distributions can improve monthly cash flow. Reinvesting can increase the number of shares or units owned. But reinvestment doesn't magically add a second return: when a fund makes a distribution, its asset value generally adjusts to reflect cash leaving the fund.

That's why the question is not simply, “How big is the monthly payment?” It's also, “How is the investment doing after the payment?” The SEC emphasizes that fund distributions and fund performance are different measurements. Read its investor bulletin.

At first, reinvesting $80 may seem insignificant. But repeated purchases can add units over time, and those extra units might themselves receive future distributions.

There is a catch worth keeping in view: a larger unit count does not guarantee a larger portfolio value. Prices, payout rates, fees, and the fund's investment strategy can all change.

Now the uncomfortable part: what if the distribution gets cut?

Let's say Alex has started relying on $120 each month to pay bills. Then the ETF reduces its cash distribution by 30%.

His $200 gross payment becomes $140. If he still allocates 60% to spending, monthly spending money falls to $84.

Alex hasn't necessarily done anything wrong. The distribution policy changed. That's precisely why fixed bills should not be built around an ETF payout as if it were a guaranteed salary.

Try a stress test: Calculate your monthly cash flow with distributions reduced by 20%, 30% and 50%. This doesn't predict a cut. It shows how exposed your budget would be if one happened.

But what about total return?

Imagine another scenario: Alex receives $2,400 in distributions during the year from an initial $30,000 ETF position. By year-end, the market value of the remaining investment is $27,000.

Ignoring taxes, contributions and trading costs, Alex has $27,000 of holdings + $2,400 in distributions = $29,400. That's $600 less than the $30,000 starting value, or a −2% simplified holding-period total return, even though he received cash all year.

Distributions are real cash. So is a decline in what the remaining units are worth. Good income planning holds both facts together.

Important distinction: Some ETF payouts are dividends, some are interest or realized gains, and some may be classified as return of capital. The label and tax character matter, especially if you're comparing different funds or account types.

What changes for U.S. and Canadian investors?

The spend-versus-reinvest choice doesn't erase tax rules.

🇺🇸 In the United States

Distributions in a taxable brokerage account can be taxable even if you automatically reinvest them. Their treatment depends on the character of the distribution. Tax-advantaged accounts have different rules.

🇨🇦 In Canada

Reinvested taxable distributions generally still need to be reported when shown on tax slips. Reinvesting can change adjusted cost base (ACB), and return of capital may reduce it. Registered accounts follow different rules.

Before setting a monthly spending target, think in terms of after-tax cash available rather than headline yield alone. See the official U.S. investor bulletin and CRA's fund-tax explanation.

Alex's four-question decision filter

Instead of copying somebody's perfect-looking reinvestment percentage from the internet, Alex asks four questions.

  1. Do I actually need the cash? If the distribution covers a meaningful expense, using part of it can be reasonable. If not, reinvestment is one option.
  2. What happens if the payment falls? Alex tests his bills against a smaller distribution, rather than treating a recent payout as permanent income.
  3. Am I evaluating the fund or just its yield? The fund's holdings, fees, payout source, strategy and total returns matter alongside the distribution rate.
  4. Where will the money sit? The tax account, currency, transaction costs and ability to reinvest fractional units can change the practical decision.

There is no universal 60/40 or 50/50 rule here. The useful habit is making the cash-flow decision consciously and revisiting it when real circumstances change.

Questions readers usually ask

Is it better to reinvest 100% of ETF distributions?

Not necessarily. Reinvesting buys more units, but it doesn't guarantee higher total returns. Investors also have cash needs, taxes, and portfolio risks to consider.

Does reinvesting distributions create extra money?

No. It changes what you own: instead of keeping distributed cash, you use it to purchase additional units. The fund's net asset value generally adjusts after it distributes cash.

Can I spend distributions without selling shares?

Often yes, if the ETF pays a cash distribution to your account. But the remaining investment value may fall, and distributions are not guaranteed.

Do I owe tax if I automatically reinvest?

Possibly. In taxable accounts, qualifying distributions may still be reportable or taxable even when reinvested. Exact treatment varies by country, account, and distribution character.

What if I only have $5,000 invested?

You can use the same percentage-based planning process. At an illustrative 8% annual distribution rate, $5,000 corresponds to about $33.33 per month before tax, if the rate stays constant. A smaller amount can still teach useful habits.

The real win isn't choosing the perfect percentage

By the end of the evening, Alex hasn't discovered a secret ETF that pays forever. He's done something better: he knows what his distributions are supposed to accomplish.

A portion can make the present a little easier. Another portion can buy additional units for the future. And both choices make more sense when he's tracking what happens to the whole investment, not just the next payment notification.

On his desk, Alex writes down one reminder: "Income is useful. A plan makes it useful on purpose."
